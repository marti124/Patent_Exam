The Forgotten Half of the Patent Clause

How Federal Regulations Doubled the Cost of American Living and Rewrote the Constitution

A constitutional-law litigation strategy and a citizen’s playbook — in one book.
by Roleigh Martin

The closing chapter of Volume Two, reproduced in full — on how a field can hold the answer for a century and never think to ask the question.

Every reader of this work eventually arrives at the same question, and it is a fair one: why is this argument coming from a retired data engineer rather than from a constitutional scholar? The chapter below answers it, and not with biography. It sets out twenty cases — in law, medicine, geology, astronomy, linguistics, and financial regulation — where the evidence was published, available, and unread for decades, and it identifies who finally asked. The pattern is not that outsiders are usually right. It is that a question lying outside a field’s settled sense of what matters does not get refused. It never comes up.

The chapter also states plainly what this work owes to a set of tools, and shows what that dependence looks like in cases where it can be checked against something physical: a word read inside a scroll nobody had opened, ink found in scan data everyone had already examined, a planet located in an archive astronomers had combed for years. In each, a machine supplied reach and a person supplied judgment — and the field’s own experts confirmed the result before it counted for anything.

CHAPTER 9

When Outsiders Advance Constitutional Law
and Other Disciplines

The Supreme Court’s own opinions, a hospital’s own death records, a journal’s own back issues, a regulator’s own files — twenty cases where the answer sat in plain sight for decades, and the people who finally asked. In the four constitutional cases, the fact that moved the doctrine came from outside the law every time.

A field can fail at its own edge in two ways.

Sometimes the field is working with what it has, and someone arrives with something better. A sharper instrument. A larger set of records. A method nobody in the field had tried. The field had tools. It did not have these tools. Call that failure the better tool.

Sometimes the field already holds everything the answer requires. The records are published. The photographs are filed. The numbers are printed in the field’s own annual report. No better instrument would help, because nothing is hidden. What is missing is a question. Call that failure the unasked question.

The two failures are not equally serious. The better tool indicts nobody. A geologist in 1900 cannot be blamed for lacking satellite imagery. The unasked question is different. It means the field held the answer, looked at it, and did not see it — and the reason was never a shortage of evidence.

The argument in these two volumes is an unasked-question case. The regulations are published in the Federal Register. Patent expiration dates are public. The window stickers are on the cars. Nothing in it required a discovery. It required somebody to ask what happens to a design when the patent runs out and the federal standard does not.

This chapter does three things. It shows how constitutional doctrine goes blind, and who has historically noticed. It shows why the person who notices almost never wins the case, which matters here because the author of these volumes cannot bring one. Then it turns to a group of results from the last fifteen years in which no human being was holding the blindfold, because the machine was never told where to look.

Two rules run through all of it. Every case gets labeled — better tool or unasked question — and where a case is some of each, the chapter says which part is which. And where the popular version of a story is wrong, the chapter gives the correction instead of quietly using the half that helps. Rounding a case toward the answer you want is the exact error this chapter accuses other fields of making.

Thomas Kuhn, 1962 — A name for the blind spot

Thomas Kuhn gave this problem its vocabulary in The Structure of Scientific Revolutions. A field operating normally does not test its own foundations. It works inside a settled framework that tells its members which questions count, which observations matter, and which anomalies deserve a second look. That framework is what makes ordinary work efficient. It is also what makes certain facts invisible while they sit in plain view.

Volume One’s preface puts the same idea in a sentence, describing pathologists who spent decades not counting the connective tissue on a cancer slide. They failed to count it, the preface says, not because they could not see it, but because they were confident they already knew which part of the slide mattered.

That is the mechanism behind every case here. Not conspiracy. Not incompetence. Not missing evidence. A settled sense of which questions belong to a field. A question outside that sense does not get refused. It never comes up.

Volume One’s chapter Why Nobody Noticed: How a Constitutional Violation Hides in Plain Sight traces how that mechanism worked in the Patent Clause case specifically — through lobbying, judicial deference, coalition incentives, the membrane between patent scholarship and constitutional scholarship, and the conformity pressure Solomon Asch measured in the 1950s. This chapter does the opposite job. It shows the same failure in medicine, geology, astronomy, linguistics and financial regulation, and it names who breaks it.

Movement One: The Unasked Question

Harry Markopolos, 1999 — The regulator would not read its own mail

Start here, because this case has the same shape as the argument in these volumes.

Harry Markopolos was a financial analyst in Boston. His firm asked him to copy a competitor’s investment returns. The competitor was Bernard Madoff. Markopolos looked at the numbers and could not copy them, because they were not possible. He has said it took him about five minutes to see that, and about four hours to build the model that proved it. Those numbers are his own, from the time, not a story he improved later.

He told the Securities and Exchange Commission. He told them in 2000. He told them again in 2001. In 2005 he sent a document titled “The World’s Largest Hedge Fund is a Fraud.” The agency did not act. Madoff’s scheme collapsed in December 2008 and took roughly sixty-five billion dollars of client money with it.

The Commission investigated itself. Its Office of Inspector General reported in August 2009. The report found that the agency had received detailed, credible complaints, had run examinations that were not adequate, and had failed to follow the warnings it was handed.

Nothing was hidden. Markopolos did not find a document. He read numbers the agency already had, that anyone could have read, and he was not an insider at the agency in any sense. He had no standing, no channel, and no way to make anyone look.

The unasked question, and note the kind. This is not a scientist missing something in nature. This is a regulator failing to see what was sitting in its own files, delivered by hand, three times.

The parallel does not need labor. The rules in these volumes are published. The patent expirations are docketed. Nobody has to find anything.

Aiko Herzig-Yoshinaga, 1983 — The government’s own file, forty years later

Aiko Herzig-Yoshinaga had no law degree, no university post, and no institutional standing at all. As a teenager she had been imprisoned at Manzanar, one of the camps where the United States held Japanese Americans during the Second World War.

In the late 1970s she was working in the National Archives in Washington. She found what forty years of litigation had not: the government’s own original copy of General DeWitt’s Final Report on the removal, containing passages that a later, revised version had taken out.

The deleted material showed the government had known there was no time to hold individual loyalty hearings. It showed that the claim of military necessity, made to the Supreme Court in 1944, had been made while contrary evidence sat in the file.

Fred Korematsu had been convicted for refusing to go. In 1983 a federal court in San Francisco threw out his conviction on a writ of coram nobis — an old and rarely used procedure that lets a court reopen a case when it learns the original proceeding was tainted. In 2018, in Trump v. Hawaii, the Supreme Court said that Korematsu had been gravely wrong the day it was decided.

The unasked question, and the most damning version of it in this chapter. The evidence was not merely available somewhere. It was in the government’s own files, put there by the government, kept by the government, for forty years.

The historians in Lawrence, 2003 — A court had believed something untrue

In 1986, in Bowers v. Hardwick, the Supreme Court upheld a Georgia sodomy law. In doing so it stated that laws against homosexual conduct had ancient roots.

Seventeen years later a group of historians — George Chauncey, Nancy Cott, John D’Emilio, Estelle Freedman and others — filed a brief in Lawrence v. Texas taking that statement apart. The sources they used had been in print the whole time. The historical record did not say what Bowers said it said. The category of person Bowers described did not exist in the period Bowers pointed to.

The Court overruled Bowers. Lawrence held that the Constitution protects the private conduct that Bowers had let states make criminal.

The unasked question, and the cleanest one in law. Nothing was discovered. A court had asserted a fact, everyone had believed it, and for seventeen years nobody checked it against a body of scholarship that already said otherwise.

Josephine Goldmark, 1908 — An outsider hands the Court a library

Josephine Goldmark was the research director of the National Consumers League. She had no law degree.

Oregon had a law limiting women in factories and laundries to ten hours a day. An employer challenged it. The brief filed for Oregon went up under the name of Louis Brandeis, then a practicing lawyer and later a Justice of the Supreme Court. It ran a hundred and thirteen pages. Goldmark and her researchers filled about ninety-eight of them with factory inspection reports, medical testimony, and labor statutes from other countries. Roughly two pages were legal argument.

The Court upheld the law in Muller v. Oregon. More striking, it took formal notice of the material. A brief of this kind is now called a Brandeis brief, and it is the ancestor of every filing that brings a court facts rather than precedent.

Two corrections belong beside the praise.

The legal work in Muller existed. It was simply in a different document, because Oregon’s attorney general filed a conventional brief with the precedents in it. And some of the medical evidence Goldmark gathered was later challenged and refuted.

The bigger problem is what the case was used for. Muller rested on the idea that women’s dependence justified protection men did not get, and that idea did real harm for two generations. Praise the method. Do not praise the holding.

The unasked question. The reports Goldmark compiled were public. Nobody had put them in front of a court.

Kenneth and Mamie Clark, 1954 — A sentence the Court could write

The story most people know is that the Clarks’ doll studies decided Brown v. Board of Education. They did not.

Kenneth and Mamie Clark were psychologists. Neither was a lawyer. Their work appears in footnote eleven of the opinion, in a list of social science sources offered to support a conclusion the Court had already reached on constitutional grounds. The footnote became famous because it was attacked, not because the case turned on it.

What the Clarks did supply was a finding the Court was willing to state: that segregation produced a sense of inferiority in children, and that the feeling affected their learning. That had been observable for decades.

The unasked question, with the causal claim kept small. The Clarks did not win Brown. They gave the Court a sentence it could write.

Ignaz Semmelweis, 1847 — A controlled trial nobody read as one

The Vienna General Hospital ran two maternity clinics. Doctors and medical students staffed the first. Midwives staffed the second.

From 1840 through 1846 the death rate from childbed fever in the doctors’ clinic was 98.4 per thousand births. In the midwives’ clinic it was 36.2. The figures were in the hospital’s own ledgers and printed in its annual returns.

Now the part that makes this unanswerable. Women were admitted to the two clinics on alternate days, with no medical selection of any kind. So the obvious objection is not available. The doctors’ clinic was not taking sicker patients. The only systematic difference between the two groups of women was who delivered them.

The hospital was running a controlled experiment without meaning to, publishing the results every year, and reading them as nothing.

Ignaz Semmelweis saw it in 1847, after a colleague named Jakob Kolletschka cut himself during an autopsy and died of what Semmelweis recognized as the same disease killing the mothers. The doctors came to deliveries straight from the dissecting room. The midwives did not. Semmelweis ordered chlorine handwashing that May. Deaths fell.

The second correction matters more. Semmelweis was not an outsider. He was a credentialed physician, an assistant in the very clinic whose figures he read. If a theory of blindness requires an outsider to break it, that theory deletes the best case medicine has. The blindness here was not caused by a shortage of credentials. It was caused by a settled sense of what those mortality figures were about.

The unasked question.

Joseph Goldberger, 1914 — The observation that needed no instrument

Pellagra killed thousands of Americans a year in the early twentieth century, most of them poor and most of them Southern. The medical consensus was that it was infectious. A commission had said so. An insect carrier was proposed.

Joseph Goldberger of the Public Health Service made an observation that required no instrument at all. In the asylums and orphanages where inmates fell ill by the hundreds, the staff never did. An infection does not respect a payroll. So it was not an infection. It was diet.

He published the reasoning in 1914. Every person who worked in those institutions could have made the same observation, and the institutions kept the records.

The resistance is the useful part, because its stated ground was not medical. Southern physicians, newspapers and officials rejected a finding that blamed regional poverty. The objection was not to the evidence. It was to what the answer implied about them.

The unasked question. The evidence was in the buildings where the disease was.

C. Henry Kempe, 1962 — Fifteen years of X-rays, then fifty state laws in five

In 1946 a radiologist named John Caffey described a pattern in infants: multiple broken bones, healing at visibly different times, together with bleeding around the brain. Bones do not break in that pattern by accident.

The films kept coming. They sat in hospital record rooms across the country for sixteen years.

In 1962 C. Henry Kempe and four colleagues published a paper in the Journal of the American Medical Association called “The Battered-Child Syndrome,” and named what the films showed.

Then something happened that has no parallel elsewhere in this chapter. The first state laws requiring doctors to report suspected abuse passed in 1963. By 1967 all fifty states had one.

The unasked question, with a sixteen-year gap and a five-year legislative answer. It is the one case here where a blindness was corrected and the law rebuilt inside a decade.

Yuri Knorozov, 1952 — The key had been in print for eighty-eight years

This is the purest case in the chapter, and it is not the case usually told.

In the sixteenth century a Spanish friar named Diego de Landa wrote down what he took to be the Maya alphabet. His manuscript was published in Paris in 1864. Photographic copies of the surviving Maya books were in print by the end of that century. Everything needed was public.

Scholars used it. They failed. De Landa’s list is inconsistent, some of its signs never appear in real inscriptions, and — above all — everyone read it as an alphabet.

In 1952 Yuri Knorozov, working in Leningrad, read it as a syllabary instead. When de Landa asked his Maya informant for the letter B, the informant gave him the syllable be. Knorozov added a rule about how syllables pair, and the writing began to open.

Eric Thompson, who dominated Maya studies, held that the script carried ideas rather than sounds, and he resisted for decades. The fight was personal as well as scholarly, and Knorozov was disparaged for working in the Soviet Union. Acceptance came through the 1960s and 1970s. Michael Coe later wrote that everyone working on Maya had become, in a sense, a follower of Knorozov.

Now the correction, and it makes the case stronger rather than weaker. It is not true that nobody asked. Many asked. What Knorozov brought was not evidence and not an instrument. It was a different idea about what kind of writing it was.

That is exactly the shape of the argument in these volumes. The Patent Clause has been read continuously since 1787. Reading a text wrongly is not the same as never reading it. That is why forgetting is the right word for what happened to the Clause’s second half.

The unasked question, and the strongest example of it here.

Robin Warren and Barry Marshall, 1979 — Counted, then explained away

In 1893 an Italian scientist named Giulio Bizzozero described spiral organisms living in the glands of the stomach. He was looking at dogs.

The human record came later and it is worse. In 1938 a researcher named Doenges examined 242 human stomachs after death and found the same spiral organisms in forty-three percent of them. He connected them to no disease. In 1940 Freedberg and Baron found them in living patients; the result was never repeated, and no one published a failure to repeat it either.

Then in 1954 Palmer studied stomach tissue from a thousand people and concluded the organisms were contamination carried in from the mouth. Medicine already believed the stomach was sterile, because stomach acid kills bacteria. Palmer’s study told the field what it already believed, on a sample too large to argue with. The question closed for thirty years.

Robin Warren, a pathologist in Perth, took the organisms seriously again in 1979. Barry Marshall joined him in 1981.

This case must be labeled honestly, and the honest label is mixed. What broke the resistance was not the argument. It was a run of new capability. The bacterium was finally grown in 1982, after culture plates were left in the incubator over a long weekend instead of the usual two days. Marshall drank a dose of it in 1984 and made himself ill, which satisfied the classical test for proving a microbe causes a disease. Clinical trials followed. The Nobel Prize came in 2005.

So the slides were an unasked question for ninety years, and the answer came from the better tool. Both halves are true and the chapter needs both.

The sharper point is this. The organisms were not merely present and unremarked. They had been counted, in a published series, in nearly half a large sample — and then explained away.

J Harlen Bretz, 1923 — A landscape in plain sight

Eastern Washington holds a landscape that looks wrong: dry waterfalls hundreds of feet high, gravel bars the size of hills, a basalt plain scoured bare. J Harlen Bretz read it as the work of a single catastrophic flood and published that in 1923.

Geology at the time treated catastrophe as disreputable. The discipline had been built on the principle that landscapes form slowly, by processes still running today, and a flood argument sounded like a return to Noah. In January 1927 the Geological Society of Washington arranged a meeting where senior geologists — several of whom had never visited the ground — took his argument apart in turn.

The landscape had been there the whole time. It was mapped.

What eventually settled it was the water. Bretz could not say where a flood that size would come from. Joseph Pardee could: he showed in 1942 that a glacial lake in Montana had drained catastrophically, and the ripple marks proved it. General acceptance came in the 1960s and 1970s. Bretz received his profession’s highest award in 1979, at ninety-six.

The unasked question about the landscape, with a missing fact that Bretz himself never supplied. Roughly fifty years.

Fritz Zwicky, 1933 — Arithmetic anyone could have done

Fritz Zwicky measured how fast galaxies were moving inside the Coma cluster and compared that with how much matter he could see. The galaxies were moving far too fast. At those speeds the cluster should have flown apart long ago. Something invisible was holding it together, and there was a great deal of it. He named the missing material dunkle Materie — dark matter.

The calculation used published measurements and standard physics. Any competent astronomer could have run it.

It was largely neglected for about forty years. Not entirely ignored — Sinclair Smith found a similar discrepancy in another cluster in 1936 — but no consensus formed. Zwicky was abrasive, which did not help, and one number he relied on was later revised.

Acceptance came when Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured how fast stars orbit inside individual galaxies, starting with Andromeda in 1970, and when radio astronomers reached further out than optical telescopes could.

Mixed. The 1933 result was an unasked question. The 1970s resolution took better tools.

Ruth Gilbert and the sleeping-position reviewers, 2005 — Forty-five years of confident wrong advice

For most of the twentieth century, parents in the English-speaking world were told to put babies to sleep on their fronts. It was in the childcare books from 1943 onward, and it was in the most trusted of them. The reasoning was that a baby lying face-up might choke on vomit.

The reasoning was never tested. A 2005 systematic review in the International Journal of Epidemiology went back through the studies and found the advice rested on extrapolation from untested theory.

Then the reviewers did the arithmetic nobody had done at the time. They pooled the studies as they appeared, year by year, and asked when the answer would have become visible.

By 1970 the pooled odds ratio for front sleeping against back sleeping was 2.93, with a confidence interval of 1.15 to 7.47. Statistically significant. Nearly three times the risk. By 1986, five studies pooled to an odds ratio of 3.00, interval 1.69 to 5.31.

The advice to sleep infants on their fronts continued to appear in childcare books until 1988. National campaigns to reverse it came in the early 1990s.

The reviewers’ estimate of the cost: a systematic review done in 1970 might have prevented more than ten thousand infant deaths in the United Kingdom alone.

And there is a sharper point in their reply to critics. Most of the loss came not from the delay in reversing the advice but from issuing it in the first place — because when British clinicians began recommending front sleeping, the two studies that addressed the question showed no benefit at all.

The unasked question, and possibly the cleanest one in this book. No instrument was ever missing. No new data had to be collected. The studies were published, in ordinary journals, on library shelves. The method that eventually found the answer was to add up what was already there. Nobody profited from the error, no institution defended it once it was shown, and no one has argued since that the reversal was wrong.

What was missing for eighteen years was the decision to look.

Sam Erman and Peter Irons, 2018 and 1999 — The nearest books to this one

No book, and nothing an open search turned up, gathers these constitutional cases into a single account of how legal doctrine goes blind and who notices.

Three qualifications, because the claim is worth less without them.

Sam Erman, in Almost Citizens (Cambridge University Press, 2018), argues that people without legal training make a difference at law. He advances it about Puerto Rico and the constitutional absorption of empire rather than about how a field goes blind, but the sentence is close to this chapter’s, and it is in print. Peter Irons — who appears above as the lawyer who used Herzig-Yoshinaga’s finding — also wrote A People’s History of the Supreme Court and The Courage of Their Convictions, which tell the people-behind-the-cases story for general readers. And Lani Guinier and Gerald Torres have argued that ordinary people, working with legal professionals, change what the Constitution means.

The limitation that matters most: no subscription law-review database was searched for this chapter. The claim is that nothing surfaced. It is not that nothing exists.

Movement Two: Why the Person Who Notices Rarely Wins the Case

Every case above has the same structure. Somebody outside the field supplied the material. Somebody inside the field carried it to a decision. The two jobs never sat in the same person.

That is not an accident of which examples I chose. Search for the pure form — an outsider whose own research produced a Supreme Court holding in a case that person brought — and you find nothing. The category appears to be empty. What follows is an account of why, built on the emptiness rather than around it.

Clarence Gideon, 1963 — The archetype that does not fit

Clarence Earl Gideon wrote his own petition to the Supreme Court in pencil and learned his law in a prison library. He is the story everyone reaches for, and he does not fit.

The Court appointed Abe Fortas to argue his case, and the winning argument was Fortas’s. The Court had also been looking for some years for a case in which to overrule Betts v. Brady. Gideon supplied the case. He did not supply the theory

Susette Kelo, 2005 — An injury, not a discovery

Susette Kelo was a nurse whose house was condemned so that a development could be built. She lost in the Supreme Court.

She found nothing and discovered nothing. What makes her case interesting is what happened after. More than forty states tightened their eminent-domain laws in response to the decision. The development was never built. Pfizer, the company the project was meant to serve, left New London in 2009.

A loss can produce a reform. Keep that in mind.

Brewster Kahle, 2007 — The closest anyone has come

Brewster Kahle is an engineer and an archivist. He built the Internet Archive. He is not a lawyer, and he made a structural observation of exactly the kind these volumes make.

When Congress removed the requirement that copyright owners renew their copyrights, it turned an opt-in system into an opt-out one. On paper, no individual term changed. In practice, works stopped entering the public domain by default, because nobody now had to do anything to keep them out of it.

That is an observation about how a system behaves, not about what a statute says. He lost.

The Ninth Circuit told him he was making the argument the Supreme Court had already rejected in Eldred, wearing different clothes.

Two things in that opinion matter here, and they cut opposite ways.

The court admitted an opening. Eldred, it said, had not directly decided whether the words “limited Times” put a real ceiling on how long a copyright can last. The Supreme Court had expressly left that question open.

Then the Ninth Circuit answered it, and answered it badly for anyone following. It held that the ceiling is found by weighing the encouragement longer terms give authors against the benefit shorter terms give the public — and that this weighing belongs to Congress, subject only to a test courts almost never fail anyone. One sentence keeps the door from shutting: future extensions may or may not survive review, but the current term is constitutional.

There is one more fact, and it turns a loss into something worse. The Solicitor General of the United States then used Kahle against the next challenger, citing it in the government’s briefs in later copyright litigation. Kahle’s defeat became a tool in the government’s hand.

The distinction these volumes rest on is that Kahle was about extending a term. The argument here alleges no extension of anything. Whether that distinction holds is for a court to say. It is stated here as this argument’s own, not as something Kahle decided.

The reason the two jobs never sit in one person is not that outsiders lack ideas. It is arithmetic and procedure.

In one recent set of 6,273 petitions to the Supreme Court, 6.3 percent were granted or sent back for reconsideration. Of the 3,785 filed by people too poor to pay the fees, 98.78 percent were denied — against 85.97 percent of the 2,488 paid petitions. A person representing himself may represent only himself. When the Court wants a case it appoints a lawyer, which separates the person who found the thing from the person who argues it, by design. And a brief that does not look like a brief is not read as one, however sound the insight underneath.

Pierre Azoulay, 2019 — What happens when the gatekeeper dies

Azoulay and two colleagues found 452 star scientists in the life sciences who died unexpectedly, and measured what happened to their fields afterward.

Publication by scientists who had never worked with the deceased rose 8.6 percent on average. Those papers were more likely than average to be heavily cited, and more likely to come from people who had not previously worked in that field at all. The barriers to entry were intellectual, social, and financial. Entry was most suppressed where the dead scientist’s close collaborators sat in positions controlling who got published and funded.

That is the mechanism of this chapter, measured. Not that outsiders are right. That gatekeeping is real, that it keeps outsiders out, and that it relaxes when the gatekeeper dies.

The docket. No reported federal decision has reached the merits of a claim that federal regulation, without extending any patent term, preserves practical exclusivity in an invention whose patent has expired. No reported decision shows such a claim being filed and thrown out on standing, on timing, or on any other ground.

The statutory neighbors point the same way, and I checked them one at a time rather than trusting a summary. The patent-term extension cases turn on what the statute means by “product.” The patent-term adjustment cases turn on arithmetic. The orphan-drug exclusivity cases turn on the phrase “same disease or condition.” The biologics cases turn on notice requirements.

In none of them was the constitutional question reached. In none of them was it raised.

Two limits on that finding, both of which a hostile reader would find anyway. It covers reported federal opinions; unpublished orders, settled cases, and state matters lie outside it. And it is a statement about decisions, not about complaints, because complaints are not collected in a database anyone can search. The claim is that no reported decision reflects the theory. It is not that nobody ever filed it.

Why has nobody argued this? A reader who has come this far should be suspicious. Generic drug makers fight regulatory exclusivity constantly, with enormous sums at stake and excellent lawyers. If exclusivity after a patent expires raised a constitutional problem, why has that industry never said so?

Three answers, and they are the honest ones.

Congress gave generic manufacturers a statutory path to market. A company with a cheaper remedy does not reach for the Constitution. The whole design of that statute is a bargain in which the industry accepted delay in exchange for a route, and firms litigate the route rather than the bargain.

Durable goods have no such path. There is no abbreviated application for a patent-expired engine. The blockage is total rather than temporary, which is exactly why it raises a question the drug cases never had to reach.

And a would-be remanufacturer stands in a worse procedural position than a generic filer. A drug company with an application pending has a concrete injury on a known timetable. Someone who would build a patent-expired vehicle if the rules allowed it has to establish that the injury is real before anything has been built.

That is a reason the question has gone unasked. It is not a reason the answer is no.

What this means for these volumes. This is why the book is written as a brief for a plaintiff rather than as a complaint. The author found the argument. The author cannot carry it. Every case in this movement says that the person who sees the thing and the person who wins the case are two different people. The honest response is to write for the second person, not to pretend to be him.

Adoption is the verification. That is mechanics, not modesty.

Movement Three: When the Machine Asked

Volume One’s preface names three people who used machine learning on a field they had no standing in. It gives them a paragraph each, which is all a preface can afford. They deserve more, because between them they show exactly what the arrangement behind this book looks like when it can be checked against something physical — and because the preface files all three under the same heading, which is not right.

This movement takes them first, then turns to something else.

Casey Handmer, 2023 — Ink in a scan a thousand people had already opened

In AD 79 Vesuvius buried the town of Herculaneum, and with it a villa library of roughly 1,800 papyrus scrolls. The eruption cooked them to charcoal. They survived because they were carbonized, and they became unreadable for the same reason: the ink is carbon, the papyrus is carbon, and unrolling one destroys it. For two and a half centuries they sat in Naples as illegible black logs.

In 2019 Brent Seales at the University of Kentucky put two of them through a particle accelerator and produced X-ray scans at resolutions down to a few micrometres. Each scroll ran to about five terabytes of data holding perhaps ten thousand words. In 2023 Nat Friedman and Daniel Gross turned the problem into an open competition, released the scans publicly, and put up a grand prize of seven hundred thousand dollars.

So the data was on the internet. Anyone could download it. Hundreds of people did.

Casey Handmer downloaded it too. He is a physicist, formerly at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory, and he had no training in papyrology, no classical Greek, and no connection to the field. He also did not build anything. He opened the flattened scan segments and looked at them, for hours, the way you would look at anything you did not understand.

And he noticed a texture. A faint pattern in the papyrus surface that resembled cracked mud, appearing in shapes that looked like Greek letters. He called it crackle, wrote it up on his own blog in August 2023, and published it.

The carbon ink was invisible because it has almost the same X-ray density as the carbon papyrus. What Handmer saw was not the ink. It was the disturbance the ink had left in the surface it dried on — the mark of a thing rather than the thing.

It was the first substantial evidence of ink anyone had found inside an unopened scroll. The competition awarded him ten thousand dollars.

The unasked question, in its purest modern form. No instrument was missing. The scans were public, high resolution, and in the hands of hundreds of contestants and the professional papyrological community alike. Handmer used no model, no algorithm, and no equipment. He looked at a picture everyone had, for longer than anyone else had, without knowing what he was supposed to see — which is precisely why he saw it.

Handmer himself calls it his modest contribution. It was the hinge on which everything after it turned.

Luke Farritor, 2023 — What the machine did with what the man noticed

Luke Farritor was twenty-one, a computer science undergraduate at the University of Nebraska–Lincoln. He read Handmer’s blog post.

Then he did the thing Handmer could not. He took the crackle pattern as training data, built a machine-learning model to find more of it, fed the letters it found back in as further training, and iterated. The model began returning ink strokes. Then characters. Then, in a patch of scroll about four square centimetres across, more than ten of them in sequence.

The word was πορφύρας — porphyras — Greek for purple, or cloths of purple. It was the first word ever read from the inside of an unopened Herculaneum scroll. Farritor won forty thousand dollars in October 2023. A second contestant, Youssef Nader, reached the same word independently and took ten thousand.

By the end of December, Farritor, Nader and Julian Schilliger had together recovered about two thousand characters, roughly five percent of the scroll, and shared the seven-hundred-thousand-dollar grand prize.

And then papyrologists read the Greek. Scholars at Naples, at Pisa, and elsewhere confirmed the readings and worked out what the text was.

This one is the better tool, and the labels matter here more than anywhere else in the chapter, because the sequence is the point.

Seales built the instrument. Handmer, who had no standing at all, noticed something in data everyone possessed. Farritor, who had no standing either, built a machine that could find more of what Handmer noticed. And the field’s own experts read the result and said whether it was Greek.

Four steps. An institution supplied the capability. An outsider supplied the observation. A second outsider supplied the machine. Insiders supplied the judgment about whether any of it was true.

That is the arrangement behind these two volumes, and it is worth saying plainly rather than leaving a reader to infer it. The author of this book could not have assembled its argument without machine assistance, and does not claim otherwise. What the Herculaneum sequence shows is that the assistance is not the whole of it, and never was. Somebody has to notice. Somebody else has to check. The middle step is the only one a machine performs.

Christopher Shallue, 2017 — A planet in an archive that had been combed

NASA’s Kepler telescope watched roughly 150,000 stars at once, looking for the minute dimming that occurs when a planet crosses in front of one. It produced an archive that was public, enormous, and worked over for years by professional astronomers who had extracted thousands of planets from it.

Christopher Shallue was a software engineer at Google. He was not an astronomer. By his own account he went looking for the data in his spare time, having read that astronomy had more of it than people to sift it.

The professionals had concentrated on strong signals, for a defensible reason: a weak dip in a star’s brightness is usually noise, and chasing weak signals wastes time on false positives. Shallue’s idea was that a neural network might be able to sort the weak ones — that the constraint had been human attention rather than the data.

He trained a network on fifteen thousand Kepler signals that human scientists had already labelled as real planets or false alarms. On a held-out test set it classified them correctly ninety-six percent of the time. Then he and Andrew Vanderburg pointed it at 670 star systems already known to hold multiple planets, on the reasoning that a system with several planets was a likely place to find another.

It found one. Kepler-90i — about thirty percent larger than Earth, orbiting its star every 14.4 days, with a surface temperature above eight hundred degrees Fahrenheit. Kepler-90 had been identified in 2013 as the first seven-planet system Kepler found. The eighth planet’s signal was too weak for the methods that found the other seven. That made Kepler-90 the first system known to match our own for planet count.

The same run turned up Kepler-80g as well.

Andrew Vanderburg was a NASA Sagan Fellow at the University of Texas, and he was the astronomer. He worked the problem alongside Shallue, co-authored the paper, and supplied what Shallue could not: the domain judgment about where to look and which candidates survived scrutiny. His description of the method is the best sentence anyone produced about it — sifting rocks to find jewels, where a finer sieve catches more rocks but may catch more jewels too.

The paper ran in The Astronomical Journal in February 2018. Kepler-90i sits in the NASA Exoplanet Archive alongside every other confirmed world.

A hybrid, and the halves are worth separating. The network was a new tool and Shallue built it. But the tool is not what had been missing. The data was public and had been examined. What had not happened was anyone asking whether the signals the professionals had reasonably skipped were worth a second look. The question was the contribution. The network was how he could afford to ask it.

None of these three men could have done what an astronomer, a papyrologist or a pathologist does. Every one of them needed one to confirm the result. What each supplied was a question the field had not put to its own evidence, and a machine capable of putting it.

In the results below, the person holding the instrument was an insider — fully credentialed, working on data his own field had been generating for decades. The machine found something in that data that the field did not know was there. Nobody asked it to look, because nobody knew there was anything to look for.

That takes the operator’s credentials out of the argument entirely. These are not stories about clever outsiders. They are demonstrations that a field can stare at its own evidence for a century and not see what is in it.

Ryan Poplin, 2018 — Sex and heart disease in a photograph of the eye

Doctors have been photographing the back of the eye for about a hundred years. The picture shows the retina and its blood vessels, and it is used to look for eye disease.

Poplin and his colleagues trained a system on retinal photographs from 284,335 patients and tested it on two separate groups. From the photograph alone, the system estimated a patient’s age to within about three and a quarter years. It read blood pressure to within about eleven points. It predicted whether the patient smoked, and whether the patient would have a major cardiac event.

It also identified the patient’s sex, almost perfectly.

No ophthalmologist can do that. No ophthalmologist suspected it could be done. The authors say plainly that these signals were not previously thought to be present in retinal images at all.

The unasked question, without qualification. The instrument was new. The information was not.

Andrew Beck, 2011 — The background was the signal

Volume One’s preface tells this story briefly. It earns a fuller telling here, because it is the case the whole book is built on.

Researchers at Stanford built a system called C-Path and turned it on breast-cancer tissue slides, measuring 6,642 separate features of each image. Breast cancer had been graded for decades by reading the epithelium — the cancer cells themselves — under a scheme largely unchanged since the 1920s.

C-Path found that features of the stroma, the ordinary connective tissue surrounding the tumour, predicted how long a patient would live more strongly than the features of the tumour did.

The finding was built on one hospital cohort, held on a second and independent one, and published in peer review.

Two corrections, because this case is usually told too well. It is partly a better-tool case: the measuring system did not exist before and had to be built using human-labelled examples. And the ninety years sometimes attached to this story belongs to the grading scheme for cancer cells. It does not describe how long the stroma went unread.

The corrected version is still the sharpest illustration in the book. Nothing hid the stroma. It sat on every slide, in plain sight, for the better part of a century — and specialists did not count it, not because they could not see it, but because they were confident they already knew which part of the slide mattered.

The unasked question. That sentence is the thesis of these two volumes, and a pathology slide is where it can be proved.

The Mayo Clinic, 2019 and 2021 — A century of heart tracings

The electrocardiogram has been recorded since the early 1900s. It is one of the most studied signals in medicine.

Mayo Clinic researchers trained a system to detect a weak-pumping heart from an ordinary twelve-lead tracing. Cardiologists do not read pumping strength off an electrocardiogram. It is measured with ultrasound.

Then the finding was tested the way findings ought to be. In a trial across 120 primary care teams at 45 clinics and hospitals, covering 22,641 adults with no history of heart failure, doctors in one group saw the system’s output and doctors in the other did not. New diagnoses within ninety days rose from 1.6 percent to 2.1 percent.

That is a modest effect and it is stated here as a modest effect. It is also a randomized trial, which almost nothing else in this movement has.

The unasked question. A century of recordings, a signal nobody thought carried this information, and no new instrument beyond the one already in every clinic.

Nicolas Coudray, 2018 — Genes visible in a stained slide

Coudray and his colleagues trained a system on lung cancer slides stained with the ordinary dyes pathologists have used since the nineteenth century. It sorted the tumors by type very accurately.

Then it did something nobody expected. From the same stained image, it predicted which of several genes were mutated in the tumor — information that normally requires sending tissue out for genetic sequencing.

Keep the calibration honest. Those predictions were moderately accurate, well below the accuracy of the sorting task. But the direction is the point. A tumor’s genetics leave a visible mark in the shape of the tissue, and a hundred and fifty years of pathologists looking at stained slides did not know it.

The unasked question.

AlphaDev and AlphaTensor, 2022 and 2023 — A different trick, often confused with this one

There is a second category of machine result, and it gets mixed up with the first constantly. It should not be, and the distinction is why this section exists.

AlphaDev searched for faster ways to sort short lists of numbers, working at the level of individual machine instructions. The routines it found were adopted into a standard library used by an enormous amount of software. Human engineers had been tuning those routines for more than a decade.

The paper also claimed that for the three-element case no shorter program existed. Cassio Neri showed otherwise. He produced counterexamples to the impossibility claim, and a plain implementation that ran faster besides.

AlphaTensor found a way to multiply certain small tables of numbers using fewer multiplications than the best method known since 1969. One qualifier is not optional: the headline result holds in a restricted arithmetic, not ordinary arithmetic.

Manuel Kauers and Jakob Moosbauer, at Johannes Kepler University in Linz, answered within about a week — and they did not merely match it. They found further schemes at the same length for the four-by-four case, then went past the machine, cutting the five-by-five case from AlphaTensor’s ninety-six multiplications to ninety-five and a rectangular format from sixty-three to sixty-two. Their method, which they call flip graphs, they devised themselves. Their computer was small and the search took seconds. And they said plainly that they had used the machine’s output as their starting point.

A leading authority on matrix multiplication called the AlphaTensor result overhyped.

Machine-generated results in game play and in pure mathematics belong in the same category.

The machine results are the better tool. A machine built something that had not existed before. That is a story about capability, and it indicts nobody. Only the first group in this movement is a story about attention, and only a story about attention says anything about how a field goes blind while holding every piece of evidence it needs.

But look at what these two cases produced, because this chapter’s own thesis turns up in the section written to exclude it — and it arrives by two different routes.

AlphaDev asserted an impossibility and was wrong. Being wrong in public is what provoked somebody to check. The error was the useful part.

AlphaTensor asserted nothing of the kind. It reported an improved upper bound, which is all anyone reports in that field; nobody knows the true cost of multiplying two matrices. What it did was show that ground the discipline had stopped digging in was not exhausted. Sixty years after Strassen, the searching had largely stopped. Within days of being shown the territory was live, mathematicians who had held the capability the whole time went further than the machine had, on a small computer, using a method of their own.

So the machine results are the better tool. The human responses to them are the unasked question. Filing the two together would be padding, and it would be the exact error this chapter has spent twenty pages describing. What the machines dislodged was not a technical barrier. In one case it was a false claim. In the other it was a settled belief that a question was closed.

The turn

In every case in the first group, the machine was not told where to look. And in every case, a human being decided what counted as proved.

Poplin’s team ran further follow-up studies to find out which part of the image the system was using. Mayo did not stop at an accurate model; they ran a randomized trial across forty-five sites. The mathematical result mentioned above arrived with a companion paper from nine mathematicians who had checked the proof by hand.

That structure is the structure of these volumes. Tools helped assemble the argument. Every case in it was read. Every figure was checked against a primary source or marked as unchecked. And a court, not a machine and not the author, will decide whether the argument stands.

Close

Fields build up blind spots at their own edges. People whose training points at those edges are more likely than average to see what is sitting there. That is the whole claim. It is narrower than the story usually told about outsiders, and it is the only version the evidence supports.

Constitutional law is a field with that kind of edge. It has the documents. It has had them the whole time. What it has not had, on this question, is the question.

The chapter on who can bring this case names the people with standing to file it. This chapter has explained why the author is not among them, and why that is the ordinary condition rather than a defect in the argument.

Goldmark did not argue Muller. The Clarks did not argue Brown. Herzig-Yoshinaga did not argue Korematsu. Markopolos did not prosecute Madoff. Each of them found something. Somebody else carried it.

The finding is here. The blindfold is described. Somebody with standing has to take it off.
